\subsection{Post-hoc Direct-Baseline Comparison}

After the locked comparison, we recognised that the liveness and saturation results had not been compared against the simplest rules aimed at the same faults. This led us to rerun the original methods with 2 direct comparators added:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Fixed repeat cap:} The gate accepts the first $R$ identical in-range readings and rejects later repeats. It resets after a change, an invalid reading or a logging gap. The cap $R\in\{2,4,8,16,32,64\}$ is shared across channels, which gives it a larger grid than the 3 values of LiveGate.
\item \textbf{Physical-bound rejection:} The gate rejects any reading exactly at either physical bound of the sensor.
\end{enumerate}
Both comparators use the original timing grid and are selected on the validation windows only. All original evaluation summary rows and selections reproduced, and the frozen artifacts remained unchanged. The follow-up is post-hoc, since the data and the original results had already been examined. Table~\ref{tab:followup} lists the selected operating points at the 0.8 target.

Physical-bound rejection issued 0.000 commands per saturation trial from an injected-fault source, while its useful-command coverage in those trials was 0.073. Thus, learned liveness was not the only rule that suppressed saturated source readings. At the 0.8 target, both the fixed-cap gate and the physical-bound gate improved overall coverage and the undesirable-command rate relative to full LiveGate. Physical-bound rejection also improved both relative to learned liveness only, although its evaluation coverage (0.798) fell slightly below 0.8. In comparison, persistence only reached a higher coverage than the fixed-cap gate at the same undesirable rate. These are point-estimate operating comparisons. They are not statistical superiority claims or evidence of field safety.

At the 0.8 target, validation selected a repeat cap of 64, 32~s persistence and 64~s maximum age for fixed repeat cap.

At the 0.8 target, validation selected 32~s persistence and 64~s maximum age for physical-bound rejection.

For stuck faults, learned liveness only reduced commands relative to persistence by 69.0 [54.3, 85.7]\% during injected-fault samples, and by 79.3 [71.0, 88.9]\% for commands using injected-fault source readings. The brackets give paired source-day 95\% intervals.

However, useful-command coverage in the same stuck trials fell from 0.982 with persistence to 0.273 with learned liveness. As such, these reductions do not establish better fault handling at matched useful-command coverage.

For saturation faults, learned liveness only reduced commands relative to persistence by 30.8 [18.0, 46.2]\% during injected-fault samples, and by 30.8 [18.0, 46.2]\% for commands using injected-fault source readings. The brackets give paired source-day 95\% intervals.

However, useful-command coverage in the same saturation trials fell from 0.918 with persistence to 0.409 with learned liveness. As such, these reductions do not establish better fault handling at matched useful-command coverage.

The 2 counters answer different questions, since a command during a fault interval can still use a held clean reading. Neither counter measures physical execution or damage. The estimates use 8 evaluation windows on 7 source-day clusters. The settings remain fixed during resampling, and the secondary intervals have no multiplicity adjustment.
